MLM2PRO vs Garmin R10: the honest comparison from someone who owns the Rapsodo

By David. I own the Rapsodo MLM2PRO and run it in my garage almost every day. I have hit plenty of balls on a Garmin R10 at a friend's setup, and I cross shopped these two hard before I bought. This page is the comparison I wanted at the time: plain numbers, subscription math included, and the small daily annoyances that spec sheets skip.

Disclosure: this site earns affiliate commissions on some buy links. The MLM2PRO in my garage came out of my own pocket, and I pay for my own membership. I hold a Lifetime Premium membership on the Rapsodo.

The short answer

Both units sit behind the ball, use radar at their core, travel well, and cost less than a single fitted driver at full price. The R10 lists at $499.99 and the MLM2PRO lists at $699.99. A $200 gap buys you a camera system and measured spin on the Rapsodo, plus a much stronger indoor story. The Garmin answers with a friendlier app ecosystem for outdoor range work, a huge watch and watch-adjacent user base, and a lower yearly membership. Indoors, in a garage, with a net or a screen, I picked the Rapsodo and I would pick it again. On a range with an open sky and a tight budget, the Garmin earns a long look.

How each unit reads a shot

The MLM2PRO pairs radar with a built-in camera. The camera watches impact, and that impact vision is a big reason the short game numbers hold up indoors. Spin rate and spin axis are measured indoors from Rapsodo RPT balls, the marked balls with printed patterns the camera can track. With regular balls indoors you still get the ball and club numbers, and carry and total distance come out as calculated figures. My driver and iron numbers indoors match my outdoor carry distances closely enough that I trust the unit for gap testing.

The R10 relies on radar alone. Radar behind the ball needs ball flight to read, and indoor flight lasts a few feet before the net ends it. The Garmin handles this well for ball speed, launch, and carry estimates, and club data comes from the radar read of the swing. In my friend's garage the R10 driver numbers looked believable and the iron numbers wandered more as the clubs got shorter. Wedges were where our two units separated. My chipping sessions on the MLM2PRO register cleanly. His R10 needed a tidy setup and some patience on short shots.

Space in a real garage

Each unit sits behind the ball, so your room math includes the space behind you plus the flight to the screen. Garmin puts the R10 6 to 8 feet behind the tee, with the tee at least 8 feet from the net. The MLM2PRO sits 6.5 to 8.5 feet behind the ball, with at least 8 feet of ball flight to the net or screen in Rapsodo setup guidance. Add 8 to 10 feet of flight and a foot or so behind the screen, and both setups want a bay near 16 to 18 feet deep. Width and ceiling follow your swing, so a comfortable driver swing calls for around 10 feet of width and 9 to 10 feet of ceiling for most golfers.

My garage bay runs tight, and the Rapsodo lives in its spot full time. Setup takes a minute: set the unit down, open the app, confirm the target line. The R10 sets up with the same simple rhythm, and its tripod legs handle uneven floors well.

The apps and the daily experience

Rapsodo's app surprised me. I bought the unit mainly as a GSPro bridge, and then the app itself won my practice time. Sessions save cleanly, video of the swing arrives with each shot, and the numbers I care about sit one tap away. I later canceled my own GSPro subscription because the Rapsodo app covered my solo practice so well. That says a lot, because I built a whole product, GolfXP, around GSPro. GSPro still rules group rounds and course play for most sim owners, and the MLM2PRO connects to GSPro on PC with the paid Core plan, the tier Rapsodo earlier sold as Premium.

Garmin's app leans on the wider Garmin Golf world. If you wear a Garmin watch on the course and keep your rounds in Garmin Golf already, the R10 slides into that habit with zero friction. Home Tee Hero gives you a large library of courses in a lighter visual style, and the range presentation feels polished. For a golfer deep in the Garmin ecosystem, that continuity has real value.

Subscription math, three years out

This section decides the purchase for a lot of buyers, so here is the math in one place. Check the data file on this site for current plan prices before you buy, because plans change.

  • MLM2PRO: $699.99 hardware. The Core plan (the tier earlier sold as Premium) lists at $199.99 per year. Core unlocks the full 15 metric set, third party simulator connections including GSPro and E6, and Rapsodo course play, and a 21 day Core trial comes with the unit. I bought the Lifetime Premium option at $599.99 list during a promotion window in October 2026. Rapsodo presented that offer as a limited run, and the window has since closed.
  • Garmin R10: $499.99 hardware, down from a $599.99 launch price in 2021. Garmin Golf membership lists at $99.99 per year, or $9.99 per month. The membership unlocks Home Tee Hero course play on more than 42,000 courses, weekly tournaments, and cloud storage for swing videos. Core metrics and the virtual range in the Garmin Golf app work with no membership.

Three year totals at list prices, membership paid yearly: MLM2PRO lands near $1,300. R10 lands near $800. The lifetime route on the Rapsodo lands near $1,300 as well in my case, with every year after year three free of membership cost. Garmin keeps the price crown across any three year window. The Rapsodo asks for more money and returns camera measured data and a stronger indoor short game.

Data each unit reports

Buyers ask me about club data most often. The MLM2PRO reports 15 metrics in total: 8 measured and 7 calculated. Club speed, club path, and angle of attack sit in the measured group, alongside ball speed, launch angle, launch direction, and spin rate and spin axis with RPT balls. Carry, total distance, smash factor, apex, and descent angle sit in the calculated group. Face angle is absent from Rapsodo's measured list for this unit, so I never quote a Rapsodo face number in my practice notes. The R10 measures club head speed directly and calculates its club numbers, including club path, face angle, and face to path. Both units cover the core ball numbers: ball speed, launch angle, launch direction, carry, and total distance, with carry and total calculated on both units.

A fair warning from ownership: treat every sub $1,000 unit as a practice tool with strong trends. Your strike pattern, your dispersion, and your gapping will be honest. A single heroic carry number on one swing deserves a shrug from either brand.

Who should buy which

Buy the MLM2PRO if your sim lives indoors, if you chip and pitch at home, if spin numbers matter for your practice, and if you plan to connect to GSPro or E6 down the road. Buy the R10 if your golf life already runs through Garmin, if most of your launch monitor time happens at an outdoor range, and if the lowest three year cost sits at the top of your list.

I own the Rapsodo, I test Rapsodo's CLM overhead unit in beta, and I still respect what Garmin built at this price. Pick the unit that fits your room and your habits, set it up carefully, and hit balls. The best launch monitor is the one that is already set up when you have 20 minutes.
