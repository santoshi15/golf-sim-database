How to pick a launch monitor for a garage sim

By David. My sim lives in a garage bay in South Dakota, and winter makes it the most used room in the house. I run a Rapsodo MLM2PRO there daily, and I am beta testing Rapsodo's CLM overhead unit in the same space. Almost every buying mistake I see in sim communities traces back to the room. The monitor was fine. The room fought it every session. Measure first, shop second, and this guide walks the order I use.

Step one: measure the room you actually have

Grab a tape measure and write down four numbers: ceiling height at the swing spot, room width, room depth from back wall to screen wall, and the distance from your hitting spot to the screen.

Ceiling height rules the driver question. As a rule of thumb from my own garage and the sims I have played in, most golfers need 9 feet at a bare minimum for a comfortable driver swing, and 10 feet feels relaxed for taller players and steep swings. Test with your own driver and your slowest, tallest practice swing while a family member watches the ceiling clearance. A plastic extension pole or a pool noodle taped to the shaft gives you a safe clearance check.

Width decides how centered you can stand. A bay near 10 feet wide works for one handedness with an offset stance. Around 12 feet lets a righty and a lefty share the room from a centered ball position. Depth decides your monitor family, and the next sections cover that math.

Step two: understand the three placements

Behind the ball: radar portables

Units such as the Garmin R10 and my MLM2PRO sit on the floor behind the ball and watch the swing and the early flight. Radar needs flight time, so these setups want solid depth: space behind the ball for the unit, plus 8 to 10 feet of flight to the screen. In return you get portability, range use on weekends, and no fixed install. Radar can pick up noise from some indoor clutter. Metal fans, fluorescent fixtures, and concrete echo all gave me shaky readings early on. My garage needed tidy cable runs and a calm background behind the screen, and then the numbers settled.

Beside the ball: floor camera units

Camera units such as SkyTrak+, the Foresight GC line, and the Uneekor EYE MINI sit beside the ball at ground level and photograph impact. They read the shot in a tiny window, so they need very little ball flight. A shallow room that starves a radar unit can suit a camera unit well. The trade lives in width and stance: the unit shares your hitting zone, left handed guests may need the unit moved across the ball, and a stray toe strike can threaten an expensive box. Place a protective case or a small barrier in your plan and your budget.

Overhead: ceiling mounted units

Overhead units, including Uneekor EYE XO class systems, the ProTee VX, and the Rapsodo CLM I am testing, mount to the ceiling above the hitting area. They leave the floor clear, serve both handedness from one install, and read club and ball data from a fixed, calibrated view. The asks: a ceiling that accepts a mount, power at the ceiling, mount height inside the maker's specified range (10 feet optimal on the Uneekor EYE XO, a 9 to 10 foot ceiling above the hitting surface on the ProTee VX, per their setup guides), and a one time install Saturday. My CLM beta has taught me how much a clear floor changes the feel of a small bay. Kids, carts, and winter clutter stop competing with the hitting zone.

Step three: match the placement to your depth

Add your numbers up honestly.

  • Radar behind the ball: unit zone behind the ball (6 to 8 feet from ball to unit for the Garmin R10, 6.5 to 8.5 feet for the MLM2PRO, and both makers want at least 8 feet of ball flight), plus your stance space, plus 8 to 10 feet of flight, plus a foot behind the screen. Comfortable total depth lands near 16 to 18 feet.
  • Floor camera beside the ball: a few feet behind the hitting zone for your backswing comfort, plus 8 to 10 feet of flight works, and tighter flight distances can work because the camera reads at impact. Comfortable total depth can start near 13 to 14 feet.
  • Overhead camera: similar depth to the floor camera layout, with ceiling height and mount placement doing the hard work.

If your depth lands under 14 feet, camera placement deserves your first look. If your depth runs 17 feet or more, every family stays in play and price plus data can lead the decision.

Step four: check your swing, your household, your habits

Handedness in the household matters more than most buyers expect. A lefty in the family doubles the value of an overhead unit or a centered radar setup. Ceiling obstructions matter too: a garage door opener rail, a beam, or a hanging heater can sit exactly where a tall backswing or an overhead mount wants to live. Stand in the bay, take your swings, and look up before you fall for any spec sheet.

Lighting deserves a line as well. Camera units rely on consistent light at impact, and many ship with guidance on brightness. Radar units care less about light and more about the electrical environment. South Dakota winters mean my garage door stays shut for months, the light stays constant, and both families behave. A bay with big windows and shifting sun may push you toward the technology that shrugs at your conditions.

Step five: decide with the three year number

Once the room cuts the field to two or three candidates, compare the full three year cost: hardware, manufacturer membership, and the simulator software you plan to run. Our compare pages carry that math for every pair in the database. Add the room parts once, honestly: mat quality matters for your joints, a cheap mat on concrete will tax your elbows and wrists over a winter, and a good hitting strip is the best money in most builds.

My garage, as a worked example

My bay gives me radar friendly depth, a ceiling that handles my driver, and a door rail that limits mount positions. The MLM2PRO earned the floor spot through daily reliability and a lifetime membership deal that ended my yearly fee. The CLM beta overhead unit is testing in the same bay now, and the clear floor keeps winning me over. If I were buying fresh for a 13 foot deep garage, I would start with floor camera units and skip a season of radar frustration. Same golfer, same swing, different room, different answer.
